Check a link before you click

Read a web address from right to left. The registered domain is the name just before the first single slash: verify-login.top in https://novabank.com.verify-login.top/secure. Everything to its left is chosen by whoever owns that domain, so novabank.com there is only decoration. Fake sites copy a real login page exactly, and the padlock only means the connection is encrypted, not that the owner is honest.

    How attackers work on people

    Technical tricks get a message into your inbox. Psychology gets it clicked. Most phishing leans on one of these levers.

    Urgency

    “Act within 24 hours or lose access.” Time pressure stops you from checking.

    Authority

    “This is the CEO”, “this is the tax office”, “this is your bank's fraud team.” People comply with titles.

    Fear

    “Suspicious login detected.” “Your parcel is held by customs.” Panic shortcuts judgment.

    Greed and curiosity

    “You won a prize.” “See who viewed your photos.” A reward or a secret is the bait.

    Trust and familiarity

    A hijacked colleague's account, or a message that names your real manager or a real invoice.

    Helpfulness

    “I'm locked out, can you reset this for me?” Good people get used because they want to help.

    Same tricks, different doors

    Spear phishing and whaling
    Messages written for one person or one senior executive, using details gathered from social media or earlier breaches.
    Smishing
    The same lure by SMS or WhatsApp, usually with a short link.
    Vishing
    Phone calls from a fake bank, helpdesk or government officer, often asking for an OTP.
    Quishing
    A QR code on an email, poster or parking sticker that leads to a fake page.
    Business email compromise
    A fake or hijacked executive or supplier email asking for a payment, gift cards or changed bank details.
    Clone phishing
    A real earlier email copied, with its link or attachment swapped for a malicious one.

    Real cases

    RSA SecurID, 2011: one spreadsheet

    Attackers emailed two small groups of employees a message titled “2011 Recruitment plan” with an Excel attachment. Opening it exploited an unpatched Flash flaw and gave them a foothold, which led to the theft of information about RSA's SecurID authentication products. Lesson: one click by one person can open the door. Be wary of unexpected attachments and patch software promptly.

    Fake invoices, 2013 to 2015: more than US$100 million

    A man set up a company named like a real hardware supplier and sent invoices to two large US technology companies (widely reported to be Google and Facebook), which paid out more than US$100 million in total. He was sentenced to five years in prison in 2019. Lesson: check any change of payment details with a call to a number you already trust, even when the invoice looks routine.

    Twitter, 2020: a phone call

    Attackers phoned Twitter employees pretending to be internal IT and steered them to a fake login page. With the stolen access they reached internal admin tools and took over high-profile accounts to run a Bitcoin scam. Twitter said 130 accounts were targeted and 45 were used to tweet. Lesson: phone calls can be phishing too. Verify callers through an official channel.

    Uber, 2022: approval fatigue

    An attacker with a contractor's stolen password triggered repeated multi-factor push notifications until the contractor approved one, then messaged them on WhatsApp pretending to be Uber IT. Uber said the attacker then reached several internal systems. Lesson: never approve a prompt you did not start. Phishing-resistant keys and number matching reduce this risk.

    MGM Resorts, 2023: the help desk

    Attackers reportedly phoned the IT help desk, posed as an employee whose details were found online, and got access reset. Ransomware followed, and MGM later said the incident cost about US$100 million. Lesson: help desks need a strong identity check before resetting credentials.

    Everyday pattern: KYC, refund and parcel-fee messages

    Texts and WhatsApp messages claim a KYC check is expiring, a refund is waiting or a parcel needs a small fee. The link opens a copy of a real bank or payment page. UPI “collect” requests work the same way: a PIN is only needed to send money, never to receive it.

    Summaries rely on public company statements and court or regulator documents. Check the originals for full detail.

    Stay safe

    Check with SLAM

    • Sender: read the full address and its domain.
    • Links: hover or long-press to see the real address. Type known sites yourself.
    • Attachments: do not open unexpected ones, especially .zip, .html or Office files that ask you to enable macros.
    • Message: look for pressure, threats, rewards, and requests for passwords, OTPs or payments.

    Build good habits

    • Use a password manager. It will not fill a password on a look-alike domain.
    • Turn on MFA, preferably an authenticator app, passkey or security key.
    • Never share an OTP or PIN with anyone, including “bank staff”.
    • Confirm unusual requests through a second channel, such as the number on your card.
    • Keep your browser and system updated.

    If you already clicked

    1. Close the page and enter nothing more.
    2. If you typed a password, change it now, then change it anywhere you reused it.
    3. Reset MFA and sign out other sessions.
    4. Tell your IT or security team, or your bank, straight away.
    5. Watch your accounts for unusual activity.

    Report it

    Report suspicious email to your IT team. You can forward phishing emails to reportphishing@apwg.org. In India, report financial fraud quickly at cybercrime.gov.in or call 1930.

    Test yourself

    Decide whether each message is phishing or legitimate. You get an explanation after every answer.
